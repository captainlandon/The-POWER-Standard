Historical accountability

Judge the past without pretending it knew the future.

POWER Retrospective reconstructs public decisions using the authority, knowledge, resources, institutions, pressures, and evidence available at the time, then compares that record with what became known later.

Proposed historical pilot: the National Housing Act of 1934

The pilot would test whether POWER can connect statutory design, federal authority, institutional actors, implementation, housing markets, racialized outcomes, contemporary evidence, and later interpretation without flattening a complex history.

What a retrospective examines

DimensionCore question
Problem as understood thenHow did public actors define the condition, and whose experiences were recognized or excluded?
Authority and institutionsWhich actors possessed formal power, discretionary power, resources, and enforcement capacity?
Contemporary knowledgeWhat evidence, warnings, alternatives, and disagreements were available at the time?
Commitments and designWhat did the policy claim it would do, through which mechanisms, for whom?
ImplementationHow did rules, agencies, local actors, markets, and informal practices shape what occurred?
Outcomes and distributionWho benefited, who was burdened, what changed, and over what period?
Causal confidenceWhich effects are strongly supported, plausible, contested, or unknown?
GenealogyHow did the decision shape later laws, institutions, resources, geography, and public problems?

Then and now remain separate

The interface should allow a reader to move between two linked views. The then view reconstructs the decision environment without importing knowledge that actors could not have had. The now view includes later evidence, outcomes, scholarship, testimony, and moral or legal interpretation. Neither view erases the other.

Research safeguards

  • Primary sources and contemporaneous records remain inspectable
  • Later scholarship is labeled by date, method, and interpretive role
  • Historical actors are not credited or blamed beyond supported authority and evidence
  • Community experience and oral history can supplement, not silently replace, administrative records
  • Long causal chains display intermediate steps and competing explanations
  • Present-day categories are used carefully and explained when applied retrospectively

Why this belongs in POWER

Democratic accountability is not only prospective. Public institutions inherit laws, budgets, administrative routines, geographies, benefits, and harms from earlier decisions. A living public record should help people see those inheritances, understand how they were produced, and design present plans with greater historical intelligence.

POWER Retrospective is a proposed research module, not a completed historical finding.
